# World and Narrative Bible (Exploration Draft)

**Status: WORKING / OPEN.** This document records the thematic direction without inventing final names, chronology, factions, or metaphysical rules.

## 1. The central question

What makes a person who they are: their memories, their choices, the people who remember them, the name they answer to, or the record society keeps of them?

The game explores the gap between:
- **Self:** what a person believes about themselves.
- **Witness:** what other people remember or claim about them.
- **Record:** what books, ruins, institutions, possessions, and public history say happened.
- **Action:** what the person actually does and the consequences those actions leave behind.

These can disagree. A character may sincerely remember the past incorrectly. A written record may be accurate but incomplete. A loving relative may protect a harmful lie. A person may change so deeply that their former identity no longer describes them.

Do not reveal one universal source of truth too early. Let the player form beliefs, test them against evidence, and live with the limits of what they can know.

## 2. Names and monsters — a thematic hypothesis

Working line: **a monster is a monster when it has no name; perhaps it is destined to find its name.**

Treat this as a question for the fiction, not a simplistic rule that naming anything makes it good or harmless. A name could be recognition, a relationship, a label imposed by authority, or a way of fixing a changing person into a category.

Potential interpretations to explore:
- Some creatures lose a social identity and become difficult for humans to understand.
- Institutions define certain people as monsters to justify excluding or controlling them.
- Some dangerous beings may have motives and histories without being safe to approach.
- A person can be recognized as a person while still being responsible for harm.
- Naming may help someone regain agency—or take agency away if the name is forced upon them.

No interpretation is canon yet. Keep the world mechanism open until the owner chooses the version with the strongest narrative and gameplay possibilities.

## 3. Main story shape (provisional)

The story may begin with mundane social contradictions in a peaceful settlement: a missing person's place at a shared meal, contradictory memories of a former neighbor, an old sign with a name that nobody claims to know, or a house everyone considers empty despite evidence of recent use.

The first act should follow local consequences, not immediately expose a cosmic villain. As the player travels to new regions and dungeons, their sources of evidence broaden: family stories, public records, architecture, local rituals, political claims, and encounters with hostile or misunderstood beings.

The big mystery should eventually implicate systems that people rely on—not only a single evil person. Removing a corrupt system can still cause harm to people who depend on it. Preserving it can leave victims unrecognized.

## 4. Character truth model

Major characters should have:
- an immediate goal;
- a deeper need they may not understand;
- a fear or loss they are trying to prevent;
- information they have actually observed;
- assumptions they treat as facts;
- something they conceal and a reason for concealing it;
- a personal boundary;
- consequences they are willing or unwilling to accept.

Each character's beliefs should make sense from their lived experience, even if the player disagrees. Avoid making everyone secretly deceptive; ordinary sincerity and kindness are essential for contrast.

## 5. Moral ambiguity without false equivalence

An unresolved choice is not automatically interesting. Give both sides clear stakes, then allow evidence and consequences to matter.

- No choice must be secretly designated the developer's morally correct answer.
- A choice can protect one group while disadvantaging another.
- A sympathetic motive does not erase harm.
- Telling the truth is not always harmless, but secrecy does not become justified just because it maintains order.
- The player may lack enough information to make a perfectly informed choice.
- Consequences should grow from the setting and character motives, not arbitrary punishment.

Do not reduce the story to a single morality score. Track specific facts, relationships, promises, and outcomes.

## 6. Life simulation as narrative

The town, the player's home, friendships, marriage, and adoption/guardianship are not unrelated optional content. They give the player a lived example of identity and belonging.

A home can be an address, a deed, a place full of memories, or a group of people who make room for one another. Family members should have agency. Guardianship does not mean ownership; care should not require a child to become the person the player imagined.

## 7. Lore delivery

Prefer layered evidence over long exposition:
- environmental details and spatial arrangement;
- NPC stories that differ in emphasis or memory;
- records whose authorship, date, and purpose matter;
- objects with evidence of use or repair;
- local ceremonies and everyday phrases;
- enemy behavior that hints at habits or attachments;
- architecture that reveals the priorities of whoever built it.

Give players enough context to form a theory, but leave room for reasonable disagreement. Optional lore should deepen the experience without making basic navigation or combat depend on reading every note.

## 8. Canon policy

No final names, factions, gods, chronology, magic rules, character biographies, romance routes, or endings are canon by this document. Use OPEN / WORKING / LOCKED labels in later design records. Only the project owner can lock major creative decisions.
