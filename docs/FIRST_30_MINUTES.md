# First 30 Minutes — Experience Design Draft

**Status: WORKING.** This is a target experience, not a final script. The first playable should be enjoyable even before the central mystery is understood.

## Success condition

At minute 30, the player should:
- recognize the town's basic layout;
- remember at least three people for different reasons;
- understand one small local problem;
- have contributed in a modest but visible way;
- have a temporary place to sleep;
- want to return the next morning;
- have noticed one quiet inconsistency worth investigating.

Do not start with a prophecy, full lore lecture, forced combat tutorial, or urgent world-ending threat.

## Beat outline

### 00–05: Arrival

The traveler approaches the valley along a winding path. The town and distant castle are visible in the same vista. The player can walk, look around, inspect a sign, and approach the settlement without the camera constantly forcing attention.

A practical inconvenience—lost directions, a damaged travel item, a cart blocking the lane—creates the first low-pressure interaction. The player is not automatically welcomed as special.

### 05–12: Observe and meet

Introduce a small cast through places where they naturally spend time: a market stall, a riverside work area, a communal noticeboard, and a public gathering spot. Use schedules and environmental staging to imply that their lives predate the player's arrival.

Allow the player to notice small social differences. Two NPCs may interpret a local issue differently without immediately arguing or revealing a secret.

### 12–20: Contribute

A manageable local need gives the player a chance to help: secure something before rain, carry supplies, repair a simple obstruction, or assist with preparations for an evening meal. Prefer one flexible task with clear feedback over a checklist of many arbitrary collectibles.

The result should be visible in the world. An NPC comments on the help, an object changes state, or the evening gathering becomes possible.

### 20–26: Earn a place to sleep

The town offers temporary shelter in exchange for a useful contribution or a small, understandable obligation. The player's room is modest and interactive: enter, inspect a few objects, place one personal item, and rest. This is not permanent house ownership.

### 26–30: The first inconsistency

At an ordinary evening meal, small festival, or local ritual, a resident mentions someone whose existence or name others remember differently. Reactions should be subtle: a pause, a change of subject, an empty place setting, or a disagreement that nobody wants to pursue in public.

No immediate attack or melodramatic cliffhanger is required. The town remains peaceful. The player has a concrete reason to ask questions tomorrow.

## Onboarding principles

- Teach controls through natural actions and optional prompts.
- Avoid high-pressure timers in the opening.
- Make core pathing obvious through composition, light, and landmarks.
- Let players wander briefly without making them miss the story.
- Use direct interaction feedback; never require a pixel-perfect cursor.
- Build a short loop from movement to conversation to world change to rest.
- The opening should still work if players skip optional dialogue.

## First-slice cut line

Required: player movement/camera, town traversal, interaction prompt, dialogue, one simple task, temporary shelter, NPC schedule slice, time-of-day transition or clear rest transition, one narrative inconsistency, save/reload smoke test.

Deferred: farming calendar, marriage, adoption, complex economy, full combat progression, procedural world generation, dungeon-scale architecture.
